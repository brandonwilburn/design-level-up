A bookshop, in two rooms

The building is from 1843.
The bookshop is from 2014.

Henry opened the bookshop in the front room of an old brick building on Exchange Street in October 2014. He had three hundred and twelve used books, a card table, and a coffee maker that he had borrowed from his mother. The first customer bought a paperback copy of "The Shipping News" for four dollars. Henry remembers this.

The front room is the new books and the signed first editions. The back room is the used books, the poetry, and the small press. The back room is also where the reading groups meet, on a long oak table that predates the bookshop by about a hundred and twenty years.

The bookshop is small by the standards of chain stores and by the standards of any reasonable business model. We carry about eleven thousand four hundred titles at any given time, which is a fraction of what a Barnes & Noble carries in a single store. We think the constraint is a feature. Every book on these shelves is a book that one of us has read, and one of us has argued about, and one of us has decided belongs here.

We don't have a recommendation algorithm. We have four people who work here, and they all read different things, and they all argue about books, and the arguing is the point. If you walk in and ask for a book, you will get a book. If you walk in and don't know what you want, you will get a book and a five-minute conversation about it. This is the entire business model.

A bookshop is a
recommendation engine
that runs on coffee
and disagreement. — Henry, on opening day

We host about forty author events a year. Most of them are in the back room, around the oak table, with about thirty people. Some of them are in the front room, with about eighty. The authors who come to Lighthouse tend to come back. We have a small blackboard in the front window that lists the next four events, in chalk, in Henry's handwriting.

P.S. — We are closed on Christmas Day, Thanksgiving, and the first Wednesday of every month. The Wednesday closing is so the four of us can go for a walk. The walk is not optional.

No. 09 · August 2026

On the difference between a bookshop and a bookstore.

By Henry Ashby

A bookshop is run by a person who has opinions about books. A bookstore is run by a person who has opinions about inventory. I have thought about this for eleven years, and I have not changed my mind. The opinions are the product. The inventory is the substrate. If you remove the opinions, you have a warehouse with better lighting.

The way you can tell, walking in, is the front table. A bookshop's front table changes every Tuesday, sometimes twice a week. A bookstore's front table changes on a corporate schedule, with seasonal resets. The front table of a bookshop is the bookseller's weekly essay about what they want the reader to read next. The front table of a bookstore is a list of books the chain has been told to push.

We put seven books on our front table this week. I am, at this moment, willing to argue that any one of them is the most important book on the table. This is, perhaps, an argument for staffing a bookshop with people who read.

No. 08 · June 2026

On the reading groups, and on the oak table.

By Henry Ashby

We have six reading groups. They meet on alternating Fridays, around an oak table in the back room that I bought from a woman in Yarmouth in 2015, for four hundred dollars. The table seats twelve and is older than the building. It came out of a schoolhouse, which is the right provenance for a table around which people are trying to learn things.

The reading groups have been meeting for most of the bookshop's life. The longest- running one, the Tuesday night fiction group, has been going since 2014. People have come and gone, but the table has not. The Tuesday group has read, by my count, about ninety books. They have agreed about maybe eleven of them. This is, I think, a good ratio.

The hardest thing about hosting a reading group is not picking the books. It is keeping the table from being a place where everyone is polite. The point of a reading group is to argue about a book, in the company of people who have also read the book. The argument is the work. The book is the excuse.
