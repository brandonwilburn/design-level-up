Used vinyl, organized by what we like

The
backstock.

We organize the used section by what we like, not alphabetically. A jazz bin, a soul bin, a hip-hop bin, a Detroit bin, a house bin, a "weird" bin, and a $5 bin. The $5 bin is the second-most-popular section of the shop.

I

The Jazz Bin

About 1,400 records

Hard bop, post-bop, spiritual, free. Blue Note, Prestige, Impulse!, Riverside, ESP. We dig through estate sales on the east side. The Coltrane section is the deepest in the shop.

"If you want to learn jazz, start in the middle of the Coltrane section and read every album from left to right." — Otis

II

The Soul Bin

About 900 records

Northern soul, deep soul, Memphis soul, Motown. The 45s section is, if we do say so ourselves, the best in Detroit. We have a 45 of "Tainted Love" by Gloria Jones on the original UK pressing.

III

The Hip-Hop Bin

About 1,100 records

Golden age, underground, Detroit-specific (yes, we have a J Dilla sub-bin, obviously). The original pressing of "Paul's Boutique" goes for about $120 these days. We have two.

IV

The Detroit Bin

About 600 records

Techno, house, and the more experimental end of the Detroit electronic scene. UR, Metroplex, Transmat, Planet E, KMS. This bin is the reason the shop exists.

"Detroit techno is the local music. We are a Detroit record store. This bin is the most important bin we have."

V

The House Bin

About 500 records

Chicago, New York, Detroit, London, Berlin. Deep house, acid, garage. We have a 12" of "Can You Feel It" on the original pressing. We will probably never sell it.

VI

The Weird Bin

About 300 records

We don't really know how to categorize this bin. It is mostly ambient, some library music, some soundtrack, a surprising amount of Ethiopian jazz.

VII

The $5 Bin

About 1,200 records

The second-most popular section of the shop. Records that came in with light wear, records that have been here too long, records we accidentally bought two of. All $5. None over $5. Ever.

No. 11 · August 2026

On the Dilla reissue, and on what we owe the records.

By Otis Reed

The new "Donuts" reissue came in last Friday. The heavyweight pressing, the tip-on sleeve, the poster. We sold nineteen copies over the weekend, which is a lot for a record that came out in 2006. Most of the people who bought it already own the old one. Some of them owned two old ones. They bought the reissue because it is a better pressing, and because they want the record to last.

This is, I think, the right way to think about vinyl. The record is a thing that exists in the world. The thing can be better or worse, depending on the quality of the pressing, the quality of the mastering, the quality of the sleeve. Buying the better version is not buying the same thing twice. It is supporting the people who care enough to make the record the best version of itself.

The same is true of all the reissues we stock. A 180-gram Blue Note is not the same as the 1965 original. The 1965 original is the document; the reissue is the better way to hear it, now, on a system that did not exist in 1965. The record continues. The pressing is the next chapter.

No. 10 · June 2026

On the Detroit bin, and on what the bin is for.

By Otis Reed

The Detroit bin is, for us, the most important bin in the shop. It is also the smallest, because the records are harder to find. The original Transmat pressings from 1986 to 1991 are not in estate sales in Cleveland. They are in basements in Detroit, in the houses of the people who bought them new.

We buy from those people, when we can. We drive to the houses. We pay cash. We do not lowball. The bin is small because the supply is small. The supply is small because the records were not, in the 1980s, considered collectible. They were considered dance music. They were bought by people who were going to play them for other people, in rooms with a sound system.

The records survived because they were played. They were not put on a shelf to be saved. They were played, and they were passed around, and they were played more. The fact that they exist at all, in 2026, is because the people who owned them loved them. The bin is, in some sense, the result of that love. We are trying to be good stewards of it.
