The bigger work

We do about
thirty-eight weddings a year.

We take a small number of weddings. Each one gets a single designer from the shop, working with the couple directly, from the first call to the day itself.

June 2026 · Hudson Valley

Ada & Mira's wedding

240 stems · 14 centerpieces · 1 ceremony arch

Two bridal bouquets (one peony, one ranunculus), fourteen low centerpieces of white anemones and sweet peas, a ceremony arch of trailing jasmine and ivy, and a hundred and twenty bud vases for the long table. The whole thing came in at about half of what the bigger shops quoted, and the flowers lasted the full weekend.

"We emailed Maya on a Tuesday. She called us back on a Wednesday. We booked her on a Friday. She did our flowers, our mothers' flowers, and the table for my grandmother's 80th the next day. I cannot recommend her enough." — Ada, on a Sunday in late June

No. 06 · Late August 2026

On dahlias, and on the long week they begin.

By Maya Castillo

The first cafe au lait dahlias of the season arrived on a Tuesday in the second week of August. They came from a grower in Orange County, NY — a woman named Sara who has been growing them for twenty-three years. The boxes were cold and the stems were long and the blooms were so pale they looked like they had been dipped in milk.

I called two of my regulars. The first said "yes, immediately, send me six." The second said "are you sure? in August?" The second regular is a smart woman. Cafe au lait dahlias in August are an exercise in controlled chaos. They open fast. They bruise if you look at them wrong. They drink water like they are trying to fill a swimming pool.

They are also the most beautiful flowers we sell. The color is impossible to describe. It is somewhere between cream and blush and a color that has no name. The center of the bloom is a soft green, like new lettuce. They last, in a vase, for about nine days, if you change the water and trim the stems every other day.

This is, more or less, what the shop is about. The flowers that are difficult. The flowers that teach you how to take care of them. The flowers that, in three weeks, will be gone, and we'll spend the rest of the year remembering them.

No. 05 · Mid-July 2026

On the growers, and on the drive.

By Maya Castillo

We buy from twelve growers within a hundred and twenty miles of the shop. Most of them are within sixty. The farthest, Sara in Orange County, is two and a half hours each way. I drive up twice a season to pick up the dahlias in person. The other eleven growers deliver to a warehouse in Long Island City where we pick up on Tuesdays and Fridays at 6:30am.

I have been to nine of the twelve farms personally. The other three are on the list for this fall. When I visit, I bring coffee and I stay for lunch. I have seen the greenhouses. I have met the dogs. I know the names of two of the growers' children.

This is, I think, the difference between a flower shop and a flower store. A flower store buys from a wholesaler in the city. A flower shop knows the person who grew the flowers. The flowers in the cooler are not anonymous. They are Sara's dahlias, and Joe's zinnias, and Tom's cosmos. They came in on a truck, from a person, from a field, an hour and a half north of here.
