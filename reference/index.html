What we stock & why

We sell what we'd
use ourselves.

That's been the rule since we opened. If Sarah wouldn't hand it to a friend, it doesn't make the shelf. Same rule applies to the coffee, the soap, the candles, the cast iron. Every item in the shop is something a person on the team actually uses at home, every week, and has used for at least a year before we agreed to stock it.

Bardstown's been on the bourbon map since 1775. We opened on the square in 2018, the same year a new distillery did. Felt right.

We started with eleven items. Seven years later we stock two hundred and forty, sourced from twenty-three makers. The list grew because the people on the team kept finding things they liked, and the makers kept getting better. The Hoffmeisters in Shelbyville, who weave our kitchen towels on a shuttle loom from 1908, started sending us two patterns a year. We now stock four.

The list did not grow because we took meetings with sales reps. It grew because Mary, who works Saturdays, found a Mennonite family in Bloomfield putting up the best pickled okra she'd eaten since her grandmother's. She brought a jar to the next team meeting. We called the family that afternoon. That jar is now No. 87 on the shelf.

If it isn't something we'd hand a friend,
it doesn't make the shelf. — Sarah, on opening day

We are not a brand. We do not have a brand book. We do not run a loyalty program, a mailing list with drip campaigns, or a TikTok. We do not advertise beyond a single blackboard on the square and a quarterly journal that costs us more to print than we make back. We are open six days a week, we pour the candles ourselves, and we know the names of most of our regulars. If you come in on a Saturday, Mary will probably be the one to hand you your bag.

Every item in the shop can be traced to a person and a place. We visit the makers. We have a file with their kids' names in it. Three of the makers below were here on opening day.

No. 14 · Spring 2026

On pouring a candle, slowly.

By Sarah Holcomb

We pour the candles on a Tuesday, because that is the day Mary is in the back room with me and we can each watch a thermometer. The wax has to come to one hundred and eighty degrees and not a degree more. Above that, the fragrance burns off. Below, it doesn't bind. This is the kind of thing I learned by getting it wrong for a year.

We use a single metal pouring pitcher, a wooden stir stick, and a 12-cup tin that belonged to my grandmother. Each taper takes about ninety seconds. We pour a hundred and forty on a good Tuesday. I never get bored of the smell of hot wax, but Mary says she could live without it.

The taper cures for seven days on a wire rack before it ever sees a label. We have lost money selling candles that weren't ready because we needed the cash, and I regret it every time. A candle is a small object. It is the only thing we make that takes a week of patience. It is the part of the shop I am most proud of, and the part that is hardest to explain to a wholesaler.

No. 13 · Winter 2025

On curing soap, and on waiting four weeks.

By Mary Tomlin

When Tom told me the soap had to cure for four weeks I thought he was joking. I had been making soap in my kitchen for twelve years and using it after two days. I was wrong about two things: it doesn't take two days, and I was using soap that was not as good as it could have been.

The four-week cure lets the lye finish its work. The bar gets harder. It lasts longer in the shower. It lathers more predictably. It smells less like the kitchen and more like the soap. None of these things are obvious in the first two weeks. All of them are obvious at four.

The hardest part is the inventory. We make a batch every other Tuesday. We have to hold four weeks of stock on the shelf at any given time. That is real money tied up in bars of soap that have no label yet. Tom says it is the cost of doing it right. I think he is right. The bars are noticeably better, and our regulars can tell the difference, even if they can't say what it is.
