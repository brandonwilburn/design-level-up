The person behind the roaster

Pete's been
roasting nineteen years.
The shop is six.

Pete trained at a roastery in San Francisco in 2007, then ran a 60kg Loring for eight years at a shop in Burlington, Vermont. He moved to Asheville in 2016, bought a 1958 Probat from a retiring roaster in Portland for fourteen hundred dollars, and opened Driftwood in 2017 on Lexington Avenue. The Probat is the same one. The sidewalk is the same one. Pete is roughly the same, but with a daughter now.

Asheville had fourteen coffee roasters when Pete opened. It has thirty-eight now. The number of farms he's visited personally has stayed at fourteen. He drives to the green bean warehouse himself.

The Probat is rated for a fifteen-kilo batch. Pete never roasts more than five. He learned this the hard way, twice, both times in 2018. Smaller batches let the drum turn more consistently, give the beans a more even development, and let Pete catch the first crack by ear. He catches it by ear. There is no computer profile.

The coffee tells you
when it's done. You
just have to listen. — Pete, on a Tuesday morning

Driftwood is not a brand. There is no subscription funnel, no loyalty program, no "story" copy on the bags. The bags are kraft paper with a hand-stamped label and a roast date. The roast date is a real date, written in ballpoint by Pete. If you email the shop asking about flavor notes, Pete writes back the same day.

P.S. — The Probat needs a new gas valve sometime this year. Pete is saving up. He refuses to put it on a GoFundMe. The savings are at about $1,400, which is, of course, what the Probat cost in the first place.

A short guide, in five lines

How to brew
a Driftwood coffee at home.

  1. 01

    Grind fresh. Every time.

    Buy whole bean. Grind what you need, right before you brew. A burr grinder, set to medium-fine for pour-over, medium for French press. The grind is the single biggest variable in the cup. It is more important than the kettle.

  2. 02

    Weigh the water. And the coffee.

    A 1:16 ratio by weight. Twenty-two grams of coffee, three hundred and fifty grams of water. A scale costs twenty dollars. It is the second-most-important thing you can buy for your kitchen.

  3. 03

    Water at 205°F. Yes, really.

    Just off the boil. If your kettle doesn't have a thermometer, bring it to a boil, then wait thirty seconds. Filtered water if your tap is hard. Pete uses the town's spring water, unfiltered, because it is excellent.

  4. 04

    Bloom for 30 seconds. Then pour.

    Wet all the grounds, then stop. The bloom is CO₂ escaping; it should bubble and dome. After thirty seconds, pour in slow concentric circles, keeping the water level constant, until you reach three hundred and fifty grams. Total brew time: about three and a half minutes.

  5. 05

    Drink it. Within twenty minutes.

    The coffee is at its best between eleven and twenty minutes after the pour finishes. After that it cools, oxidizes, and becomes a different drink. Make what you'll drink. Don't make what you'll save.

The journal

Notes from the
back of the roastery.

Pete writes a short piece every other month. No schedule, no theme, no SEO. Just whatever he is thinking about coffee, in the moment.

No. 07 · August 2026

On roasting five pounds at a time.

By Pete Holloway

The Probat will hold fifteen. For two years I roasted ten. The roasts were okay. Not bad. Not remarkable. The kind of coffee you'd drink at a conference and forget by the elevator. I think it was the batch size. Ten pounds in a fifteen-pound drum is hard to land. The air flow changes. The beans on the outside of the drum develop faster than the ones in the middle. The first crack happens at different times in different places.

I dropped to seven. That was better. Then five. Five is the number. The Probat sounds different at five — you can hear the beans tumbling against each other, the way a creek sounds. You can hear first crack without a thermometer. You can hear when the roast is done. It sounds like a small sigh.

Five pounds is half the revenue of ten. It also costs twice the time. The math does not work on paper. The math is not the point. The point is the cup. I would rather sell forty pounds of excellent coffee a week than eighty pounds of forgettable coffee. The shop is small enough that this is a decision I get to make.

P.P.S. — If you visit on a Tuesday morning, you can hear the Probat from the sidewalk. It starts at 6:14am. The first batch is always the Guatemala.

No. 06 · June 2026

On the cupping table, and on being wrong.

By Pete Holloway

Every Tuesday at noon I cup the next week's roasts. Six cups, side by side, slurped with a silver spoon, ranked. I write the rankings on a legal pad. I have been doing this for nineteen years. I am wrong about a third of the time. The customer disagrees with me about another third. The coffee disagrees with both of us about the rest.

The customer is not always right. The roaster is not always right. The cup is occasionally, magnificently wrong. A coffee that scored a 91 on Monday can taste like cardboard by Wednesday. A coffee that tasted like cardboard on Monday can taste like the best thing you've ever brewed by Friday. The cup does not owe you consistency.

The honest thing to do is tell you this. Buy the coffee because you want to try it. Drink it while it's fresh. If you don't like it, tell me. I will drink a cup with you and we will figure out what went sideways. This is, more or less, the entire business model of Driftwood.
